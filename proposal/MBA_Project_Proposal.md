# MBA Final-Year Project Proposal

## Title
**AI-Driven Operational Efficiency in the Renewable Energy Sector: A Study on Predictive Analytics, Automation, and Sustainable Business Performance**

## Abstract
Renewable energy operations face challenges from variable solar and wind generation, equipment reliability, maintenance costs, and resource allocation. This proposed MBA study examines how artificial intelligence, predictive analytics, and automation may support forecasting, maintenance planning, and managerial decisions. The study combines a voluntary questionnaire of relevant professionals, optional interviews, secondary research, and a technical demonstration comparing solar power generation forecasting models against a simple baseline. The research will evaluate adoption barriers, data readiness, and perceived operational effectiveness, while avoiding unsupported claims of causal benefits or measured savings.

**Status:** Proposal; no results have been collected.

## Problem statement
Operators need to balance intermittent generation, downtime, and operational costs. Evidence is needed to understand the feasibility and perceived business value of AI applications, as well as implementation barriers.

## Primary objective
Evaluate the potential contribution of AI to operational efficiency, cost optimization, and decision-making in renewable energy organizations.

## Secondary objectives
1. Identify AI use cases in solar and wind operations.
2. Evaluate generation forecasting using predictive analytics.
3. Explore predictive maintenance and automation.
4. Examine associations between AI adoption and perceived efficiency.
5. Identify data, skills, cost, and integration barriers.
6. Recommend a practical AI adoption framework.

## Research questions
1. How is AI currently applied in renewable energy operations?
2. Can machine learning forecast solar generation more accurately than a baseline?
3. What potential operational benefits are associated with predictive maintenance and automation?
4. Which factors enable or hinder AI adoption?
5. How should managers prioritize AI investments?

## Proposed hypotheses
- H1: AI adoption is positively associated with perceived operational efficiency.
- H2: Predictive analytics use is positively associated with perceived decision-making effectiveness.
- H3: Data readiness is positively associated with successful AI implementation.
- H4: Implementation cost and skills gaps are negatively associated with AI adoption.

These are hypotheses to test, not findings.

## Methodology
A mixed-methods exploratory/descriptive design is proposed. Primary data may come from a structured questionnaire and optional expert interviews. A target of approximately 60–100 respondents is proposed if recruitment is feasible, using purposive or convenience sampling. Survey analysis will report descriptive statistics and, where supported by sample size and assumptions, exploratory correlations or regression. Cross-sectional associations will not be presented as causal evidence.

Secondary data will include peer-reviewed literature, industry publications, and an appropriately licensed public solar generation dataset. For the technical demonstration, define a forecast horizon, clean and document the dataset, split chronologically, compare persistence or seasonal-naive forecasting with Random Forest and Gradient Boosting, and evaluate held-out predictions using MAE and RMSE. Prevent data leakage and report model limitations.

## Data collection plan
- Primary: voluntary, consent-based survey; optional interviews; anonymous or de-identified responses.
- Secondary: publicly available generation/weather datasets, research papers, and energy-sector reports.
- Potential sources: [NREL](https://www.nrel.gov/), [IRENA](https://www.irena.org/), [IEA](https://www.iea.org/), and licensed public dataset repositories.
- Dataset selection criteria: licensing, temporal coverage, measurement units, missingness, and relevance.

## Tools
Python, pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, Excel, Power BI, and Google Forms or Microsoft Forms.

## Limitations and ethics
Convenience sampling limits generalizability. Survey perceptions do not demonstrate causation. Forecast accuracy does not establish realized downtime or cost reductions. Do not publish identifiable respondent data or confidential employer/client information. Obtain university guide approval and cite sources accurately.
